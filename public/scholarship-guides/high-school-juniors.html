High-school planning guide

Scholarships for high-school juniors: where to start

Junior year is a useful time to begin scholarship research. You can identify local opportunities, learn common requirements, prepare application materials, and avoid last-minute scrambling during senior year.

Some programs accept juniors; others only accept graduating seniors or students who have enrolled in college. Each sponsor sets its own application window.

Can high-school juniors apply for scholarships?

Yes, some scholarships accept juniors, but many have grade-level or graduation-year restrictions. Read the eligibility section for each award instead of assuming that every scholarship is open to every high-school student.

Even if a scholarship will not accept applications until senior year, a junior can use the time to identify likely opportunities, prepare an activity list, and ask counselors about local awards.

Where juniors should look first

  1. Your high-school counselor. Ask for the current scholarship list and the names of local organizations that have funded graduates in previous years. Find out when the list is updated.
  2. Community foundations and civic organizations. Search county and city foundations, service clubs, local nonprofits, and community funds. Some awards are limited to residents of a particular area or graduates of certain schools.
  3. Colleges you may attend. Check admissions and financial-aid websites, plus departments connected to your intended major. Ask whether awards are automatic or require a separate application.
  4. Employers and professional groups. Check your own employer, a parent's employer, and professional or trade associations related to your interests.
  5. Government and national search resources. Use your state's higher-education agency and the U.S. Department of Labor's CareerOneStop Scholarship Finder. Confirm each result with its sponsor.

A practical junior-year timeline

  • Early junior year: map your search. List your graduation year, state or county, academic interests, activities, service, work experience, and possible college majors. Keep sensitive details out of public searches.
  • During the school year: build a shortlist. Record the sponsor, official URL, grade-level requirements, GPA rule if any, deadline, essay prompt, recommendation requirements, and whether the award is renewable.
  • Late junior year: prepare materials. Draft an activity resume, collect a transcript for reference, brainstorm essay examples, and identify teachers or mentors who may be willing to write recommendations.
  • Summer before senior year: verify current cycles. Revisit sponsor websites and check whether the application opened, the requirements changed, or the deadline moved.
  • Senior year: apply to right-fit awards. Tailor each application to its prompt and submit through the sponsor's official process. Leave time to request recommendations and records.

How to choose realistic opportunities

Do not rank scholarships by award amount alone. A national award may have a broad applicant pool, while a smaller local award may be limited to students from a few schools. Neither is automatically easier to win, but the local award may be more closely aligned with your circumstances.

Check grade level, residency, intended school or major, GPA requirement, enrollment plan, financial-need rules, and required activities or memberships. Mark missing information as “not confirmed” and ask the sponsor instead of guessing.

Watch for scholarship scams. Be cautious of guaranteed awards, pressure to pay an application or processing fee, or requests for bank details or passwords. Apply through the official sponsor website and review the Federal Trade Commission's guidance before sharing information.
